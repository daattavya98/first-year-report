`Experimental' approaches to mathematics are often naively seen as antithetical to the rigorous and formal nature of the discipline. However, if one examines the practice of working mathematicians, across various sub-fields and timescales, it becomes clear that the process of mathematical discovery is intertwined with so called `experimental' approaches.
Conjecturing is a crucial element at the heart of mathematical discovery and the ability to conjecture is often seen as a hallmark of mathematical talent. Interesting, non-trivial conjectures can drive progress for centuries and lead to unintended consequences, often birthing entire new fields of mathematics. Observing patterns in data and extrapolating from simple, well-understood cases are some of the tools used to make conjectures.
The development of computing technology has accelerated aspects of this process and human-machine collaboration has a track record of producing new mathematics. There are prominent examples of brute-force computation leading to new conjectures \cite{BIRCHSwinnertonDyer1965} and theorems \cite{4col_1977,4col_every_1977,MR2463991}.

\section{Machine guided mathematics: A modern perspective}
\label{sec:machine-guided-math}
The rise of artifical intelligence signifies a new era in mathematical discovery. Machine guided approaches have already lead to new results in areas as diverse as number theory, geometry, knot theory and string theory \cite{Davies_2021, Murmurations2024, AGGARWAL2024138517}. Crucially, many new results have been found on datasets that have existed for multiple decades which had already been actively studied. For instance, there has been an explosion in ML approaches applied to the Kreuzer-Skarke dataset \cite{kreuzer2000complete} of Calabi-Yau manifolds with implications to string geometry and string phenomenology \cite{BULL2019700,Berglund2023, hePatternsCalabiYauDistributions2015}. This dataset has existed since the late 1990s. The L-functions and modular forms database (LMFDB) \cite{lmfdb:234446.a} plays a similar role in number theory and has led to the discovery of new results about elliptic curves \cite{Murmurations2024}. The LMFDB was initially conceived in 2007. This indicates that the type of patterns spotted by AI systems on these datasets might be elusive or at the least quite different to what a working mathematician typically explores. Professor Marc Lackenby quotes ``I was not expecting to have some of my preconceptions turned on their head''
when referring to his work on AI-guided knot theory \cite{Davies_2021}.
\par However, it is unclear how to judge these discoveries and AI-guided mathematics as a whole. The recently introduced `Birch test' \cite{heTrium2024} captures some of the essence of the challenges the mathematical community faces when presented results from AI-guided mathematics. The Birch test proposes that a truly impressive AI-guided discovery must satisfy the following criteria:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Automaticity:} The discovery is completely made by a pattern-spotting AI without human intervention.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability:} Outputted mathematical statements should be clear to a human mathematician and indistinguishable from those produced by a human colleague.
    \item \textbf{Non-triviality:} Human mathematicians should come to a consensus position that the discovery is non-trivial.
\end{itemize}
Based on these criteria, the author of \cite{heTrium2024} believes that every AI-assisted discovery has fallen short of passing the Birch test so far. I argue that the Birch test is a reasonable starting point and touches upon some of the key philosphical issues when discussing mathematical discovery.
\par 
Particularly, the questions of \textbf{Interpretability} and \textbf{Non-triviality} are potentially even \textit{harder} than they first seem. In the contemporary philosophy of mathematics, it is becoming clearer that mathematical knowledge itself can emerge from an interplay between cognitive, cultural, social and historical aspects \cite{mathKnowledgeInterplayPractices2016}. The interpretability and non-triviality of a mathematical statement, discovered with the assistance of AI, is deeply intertwined with the social and historical context of the statement. It is based on the judgement of a community of mathematicians.
Currently, most AI-assisted systems are not designed to capture the social and contextual nature of judging mathematics. Similar issues are observed when validating and proving mathematical statements.
In his \citeyear{ochigameAutomatedMathematicsReconfiguration2024} article, \citeauthor{ochigameAutomatedMathematicsReconfiguration2024} \cite{ochigameAutomatedMathematicsReconfiguration2024} discusses the different `standards of proofs' of mathematical theorems from both a historical and contemporary perspective. Elucidating the `standard' that is sufficient for proof verification is an open area of research in the philosophy of mathematics. One of the central points, is that proof verification is inherently a \textit{social} process.
Borrowing a notorious example from \citeauthor{ochigameAutomatedMathematicsReconfiguration2024}, the status of the proof of the $abc$-conjecture \cite{masser1985open} is a controversial issue in modern mathematics. Different experts have their unique position on the veracity of the claimed proofs. As one approaches the research frontier, it becomes clearer that the verification of a proof relies on the trust of a group of highly specialised experts and social institutions (like journals, committees etc.). The growing importance of formal verification of proofs is partly attributable to these questions.
\par This leads to one of the central proposals of this thesis: Building a \textit{multi-agent}\footnote[1]{Here `agent' has a double meaning. It refers both to mathematical knowledge being produced by social, human agents (as introduced by \citeauthor{mathKnowledgeInterplayPractices2016} in \cite{mathKnowledgeInterplayPractices2016}) and agents in standard reinforcement learning literature.} system for mathematical discovery (See section \ref{sec:intrst-multi-agent}). 

\subsection{The impact of language models}
Recently, Deepmind has been able to achieve peformance comparable to an average International Mathematics Olympiad (IMO) gold medallist in geometry problems \cite{trinhSolvingOlympiadGeometry2024} and a silver medal performance across general IMO problems \cite{AlphaProof} by utilising language models in conjunction with mathematical datasets.
Language models are also providing new tools to the autoformalisation community, thanks to their impressive ability to handle human produced mathematical text (discussed in section \ref{sec:conj-formal}). As hinted at in the previous section (\ref{sec:machine-guided-math}), formal verification of proofs is quickly becoming a standard in the field of mathematics with certain journals requiring authors to formally verify their results at submission.
Prominent mathematicians are becoming actively involved in formalising their results and considering the impact of AI for these tasks \cite{gowers2023conjecturemarton}.

\par 
I believe that there are open questions about how transferable these methods are to the task of mathematical \textit{discovery} as opposed to their demonstrable success on formalising \textit{discovered} theorems and solving difficult problems (that are known to have solutions).
I discuss this point in further detail in the next chapter (See \ref{sec:theorem-spaces}). However, large language model (LLM) based approaches have shown success in discovering new mathematics in certain targeted tasks \cite{Romera-Paredes_2024}.
By combining a pre-trained LLM with a systematic evaluator for correctness, \citeauthor{Romera-Paredes_2024} were able to discover new constructions for the cap-set problem \cite{Grochow_2019} in extremal combinatorics. The systematic evaluator is integral to this system to tackle confabulations. To the best of my knowledge, this is the first instance of an LLM-based approach producing state-of-the-art results in \textit{research} mathematics. Moreover, their approach is highly interpretable as the construction of the new cap-set is an easily understood Python program that researchers in extremal combinatorics are analysing and learning from.
This is perhaps an example of a system that is close to satisfying the Birch test.
An important caveat is that the cap-set problem was extremely amenable to this approach as the output of the LLM could be easily evaluated for correctness. Designing such an evaluator is not an easy task for most mathematical problems and it is unclear if \citeauthor{Romera-Paredes_2024}'s approach can generalise.
However, I strongly believe that elements of their methodology are a step in the right direction and should be incorporated in future systems.

\section{Computer-aided conjectures}
\label{sec:comp-conjecturing}
Mechanised conjecturing has been theorised since at least the 1960s \cite{mechMath1960} with \citeauthor{mechMath1960} aruging that conjecturing is harder to mechanise than formalising proofs. Over the following decades, progress was made towards this goal with the development of systems like the \textit{AM, GT and Graffiti programs} \cite{lenat1976am, epsteinLearningDiscoveryOne1988, fajtlowicz1988conjectures}. Proposed by \citeauthor{lenat1976am} in his PhD thesis, the \textit{AM Program} was able to generalise existing concepts in set and number theory by utilising a number of heuristics.  
Historically, graph theory seems to have been more amenable to mechanised conjecturing. The \textit{GT program} and \textit{Graffiti program} were capable of making numerical conjectures about graphs. The tradition of mechanised conjecturing has sustained to current times. The Ramanujan machine \cite{raayoniGeneratingConjecturesFundamental2021} can make conjectures connecting rational functions to fundamental constants. All these systems are impressive feats of engineering and computation without using machine learning techniques.
An example of an AI approach to conjecturing is \citeauthor{gianniniInterpretableGraphNetworks2023}'s work on universal algebra conjectures based on interpretable graph neural networks \cite{gianniniInterpretableGraphNetworks2023}.
\par
There are a few key limitations of all the previous methods that I hope to begin addressing in my PhD:
\begin{itemize}
    \label{list:comp-conj}
    \item The systems were extremely specific to their specific domain of mathematics. The goal with our approach is to build a 'meta-mathematical system' (See \ref{sec:why-model-theory}) that is based on a methodology and heuristics that are independent of a specific mathematical area.
    \item Many of the AI techniques we have access to in the current day were simply not available to the developers of these systems. In particular, the rise of LLMs and neural reasoning systems can greatly improve machine-guided conjecturing.
    \item As pointed out in \ref{sec:machine-guided-math}, finding interesting conjectures is a social, cognitive and cultural process. It generally requires refuting, revising and proving your statements. This was also identified by \citeauthor{jiangLearningPlausibleUseful} in \cite{jiangLearningPlausibleUseful} and is a functionality that is absent in the aforementioned systems.
\end{itemize}
I aim to address these limitations through the approach I outline in this proposal.

\subsection{Method of Proofs and Refutations}
\label{sec:method-proofs-refutations}
In his seminal work \cite{Lakatos_Worrall_Zahar_1976}, \citeauthor{Lakatos_Worrall_Zahar_1976} demonstrates the circuitous path of mathematical discovery through a didactic approach. By examining the historical discovery of the Euler-Poincar\'e formula, Lakatos presents his method of proofs and refutations. As a simplification, mathematical discovery requires refuting, revising and proving your statements.
I argue that a multi-agent system which captures a process akin to this will align more to the working mathematicians' process. Statements produced in this manner are more likely to be interpretable by human mathematicians since there is a deductive chain connecting the initial and final step in a way that is parallel to human researchers.
Referring to this essay, Lakatos quotes, ``Its modest aim is to elaborate the point that informal, quasi-empirical, mathematics does not grow through a monotonous increase of the number of indubitably established theorems but through the incessant improvement of guesses by speculation and criticism\dots''.
\par The method of proof and refutations provides a pathway to incorporate the intangible aspects of mathematical discovery. It forms part of the basis of our proposed framework (c.f. \ref{subsec:MAS}).

\subsection{`Interesting' conjectures and agents}
\label{sec:intrst-conj-agents}
The ideas in the previous sections lead to a natural question. What is an `interesting' or `useful' conjecture in mathematics?
In an article published in \textit{Quanta}, Robbert Dijkgraaf attempts to capture this slippery and amorphous concept by analysing the beliefs held by the mathematical community. Good conjectures should be sufficiently hard (i.e, not easily proven). They should be deep, having far-reaching consequences that are difficult to forsee. John Conway is quoted as saying that a good conjecture should be `outrageous' and hard to believe.
What might seem surprising, is that it is often not necessary for a good conjecture to even be true! In many cases, a false conjecture that satisfies the previous criteria, might be more effective for mathematical discovery than true statements. As a more contemporary example, in \citeyear{trichotomy1984}, \citeauthor{trichotomy1984} introduced his trichotomy conjecture \cite{trichotomy1984}. Despite the counterexamples found by \citeauthor{hrushovskiNewStronglyMinimal1993} in \citeyear{hrushovskiNewStronglyMinimal1993} \cite{hrushovskiNewStronglyMinimal1993}, this is a keystone result in model theory. It has led to tremendous progress in understanding minimality and the complex field and drives research in the field decades later.
This highlights the importance of refutations.
\par Is it possible to design heuristics that can capture these notions of `interestingness'? If so, can they be incorporated into a math discovery system?
This is at the core of this thesis. I attempt to address these questions through a hybrid approach: combining a theoretical framework with empirical heuristics supported by targeted applications. There have been attempts to address this question in the past. In \cite{coltonNotionInterestingnessAutomated2000}, \citeauthor{coltonNotionInterestingnessAutomated2000} tried to isolate the commonalities and differences between the mechanised conjecturing systems detailed in section \ref{sec:comp-conjecturing}. They analysed the conjectures produced by these systems in terms of subjective criterias of interestingness.\\ For instance, consider the surprisingness of a conjecture in accordance with Conway's suggestion. 
\citeauthor{coltonNotionInterestingnessAutomated2000} discover that \textit{Graffiti} is able to estimate the surprisingness of its conjectures by keeping track of its semantic tree and utilising a certain heursitic (beagle heuristic). In contrast, the \textit{AM program} is both able to measure the surprisingness of concepts and assign extra interestingness, to concepts possessing a property not possessed by its parents.
Looking at the `utility' of conjectures, the \textit{AM program} was designed to be interactive and its heuristics were highly biased to concepts chosen by the user. On the other hand, in the \textit{Graffiti} and \textit{GT} programs, users could guide the system to only produce conjectures involving a chosen `focus' concept.
\par 
\citeauthor{coltonNotionInterestingnessAutomated2000}'s work forms a basis for the hybrid theory and applications approach I wish to explore in my PhD. The idea is to estimate interestingness heuristics by observing what is effective in specific math applications. The potential improvement to their work is to combine this empirical investigation with a theoretical framework (described in the following chapter). This can then be used to frame the goals and policies of future systems.

\section{Goals of the thesis}
\label{sec:goals}
I aim to address the following questions in my PhD:
\begin{itemize}
    \item What is an `interesting' conjecture?: Despite its impact and importance, the process of conjecturing is mysterious and there is no accepted consensus on what is a `good' versus a `bad' conjecture. 
    I aim to provide a theoretical framework for the process of conjecturing and to automate this via machine-guided tools. I intend to demonstrate its utility by discovering `interesting' conjectures in specific applications. The framework will be based on a combination of ideas from model theory, functional analysis and machine learning.
    \item How can we design a system for mathematical discovery that incorporates the process of working mathematicians and produces research level statements?: I propose that a multi-agent system would be an effective approach. Ideally such a system can address the social, cultural and cognitive aspects of mathematical discovery whilst exploiting the tremendous advancements currently being made in AI-guided mathematics.
    \item How can these tools be incorporated into the workflow of a practicing mathematician?: The goal is to publish our tools as easily accessible packages for mathematicians without requiring intricate knowledge of machine learning. 
\end{itemize}

Humans have notoriously underestimated the rate of progress of AI in impacting their field and I would argue that we are at a tipping point in modern mathematics. The arsenal of a working mathematician should include some familiarity with these tools. We are at the stage where we can influence the growth of the field of machine-guided mathematics. These questions are in the zeitgeist of the community.
